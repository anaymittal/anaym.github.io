🛰️ Aerobraking

Slowing Spacecraft Using Atmospheric Drag

What is Aerobraking?

Aerobraking is an innovative spaceflight maneuver used to slow down a spacecraft by using the drag force produced when it passes through a planet's atmosphere. Instead of relying solely on rocket propulsion—which requires significant amounts of fuel—a spacecraft strategically dips into the upper layers of the atmosphere at the low point of its orbit (called periapsis), allowing atmospheric molecules to create drag and gradually slow it down.

Key Concept: By using atmospheric drag over multiple orbital passes, spacecraft can change their orbits without burning large amounts of fuel, making missions more efficient and cost-effective.

Why Use Aerobraking?

⛽ Fuel Efficiency

Drastically reduces the amount of fuel needed for orbit changes, allowing spacecraft to carry more scientific instruments or payloads instead of extra propellant.

💰 Cost Reduction

Less fuel means smaller rockets and less expensive missions. Fuel savings can be so significant that payload mass can sometimes be doubled on interplanetary missions.

🔬 Longer Missions

Conserving fuel allows missions to last longer and complete more science, extending the operational lifetime of spacecraft.

How Does It Work?

  1. Initial Orbit: After arriving at a planet with an atmosphere, the spacecraft uses rockets for a brief burn to enter a highly elliptical orbit.
  2. Atmospheric Passes: At periapsis, the spacecraft "dips" into the thin upper atmosphere. The spacecraft may use solar panels or other surfaces to maximize drag safely while minimizing heating.
  3. Repeating the Process: Several hundred passes are usually required. Each pass slows the spacecraft a little more, gradually lowering its orbit without overheating the structure. This process can take several months to complete.
  4. Final Adjustments: Once the desired orbit is reached, the spacecraft uses a small burn to raise the lowest point of its orbit above the atmosphere, so it doesn't continue to slow down and eventually crash.

Practical Examples

Aerobraking has been successfully used in numerous space missions, particularly those exploring planets with atmospheres:

  • Mars Global Surveyor (1997-1999): First spacecraft to use aerobraking at Mars, saving an estimated 200 kg of fuel.
  • Mars Odyssey (2001-2002): Used aerobraking for 76 days to reach its science orbit.
  • Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (2006): Performed over 400 aerobraking passes to achieve its mapping orbit.
  • Magellan (1993): Successfully demonstrated aerobraking at Venus.
  • ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter (2017-2018): European Space Agency's mission used aerobraking to circularize its orbit around Mars.

Aerobraking is especially valuable for missions to Mars because the thin atmosphere provides enough drag for gradual braking without excessive heating.

Challenges

Atmospheric Uncertainty

Variations in atmospheric density, temperature, and weather patterns can affect how much drag is produced. Mission controllers must carefully monitor each pass and adjust the orbit as needed.

Heat and Pressure

Although the heating isn't as extreme as atmospheric reentry, spacecraft must be designed to withstand repeated passes through the atmosphere. The friction generates heat that must be managed carefully.

Navigation Precision

Keeping the periapsis at the correct altitude is crucial. Too low, and the spacecraft risks burning up or crashing; too high, and the aerobraking will be ineffective and take much longer.

Time Investment

Aerobraking is not a quick process. It can take several months of careful orbital adjustments, during which the spacecraft cannot perform its primary science mission.

Conclusion

Aerobraking represents an elegant solution to one of spaceflight's most fundamental challenges: changing orbits efficiently. By leveraging the natural resistance of planetary atmospheres, spacecraft can accomplish orbit modifications that would otherwise require enormous amounts of fuel. This technique has become a proven and reliable method for planetary missions, particularly to Mars, and will continue to play a crucial role in future space exploration endeavors.

In Summary: Aerobraking lets spacecraft use the natural resistance of an atmosphere to slow down instead of burning precious rocket fuel—making missions more efficient, cost-effective, and allowing for greater scientific return.
